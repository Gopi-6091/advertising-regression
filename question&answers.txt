## Stage 1 — Business Understanding

### Q1. What is the business problem?

**Answer:**
The business wants to understand how advertising expenditure across TV, Radio,
and Newspaper is associated with product sales, so the analysis can support
better advertising-budget decisions.

The data is observational, so we should not claim that advertising causes sales
to increase.

### Q2. What is the ML objective?

**Answer:**
Build a regression model to predict `sales` using `TV`, `radio`, and `newspaper`
advertising expenditure.

### Q3. Why linear regression?

**Answer:**
Sales is a continuous numerical target, and the predictors are numerical
advertising expenditures. Linear regression allows us to model approximately
linear relationships and provides interpretable coefficients.

### Q4. Why regression instead of only correlation?

**Answer:**
Correlation measures the strength and direction of a pairwise linear
association. Multiple regression allows us to estimate the association of one
predictor with sales while holding the other predictors constant.

It also gives us coefficients, confidence intervals, statistical tests, and
predictions.

### Key distinction

Correlation → pairwise association

Multiple regression → association of each predictor with the target while
accounting for the other predictors

Neither automatically establishes causation.


## Stage 2 — Data Understanding & Data Quality

### Q1. What dataset did you use?

**Answer:**
I used the Advertising dataset from the ISLR resource. It contains
200 observations with TV, Radio, Newspaper, and Sales variables,
plus an identifier-like column.

### Q2. What data-quality checks did you perform?

**Answer:**
I checked:

- Dataset structure and data types
- Missing values
- Duplicate observations
- Descriptive statistics
- Negative/impossible values
- Potential outliers
- Identifier columns

### Q3. What did you find?

**Answer:**
There were no missing values or duplicate rows, and no obvious
data-entry errors.

Two Newspaper observations were flagged as potential IQR outliers.
After investigating the complete observations, there was no evidence
that they were erroneous, so they were retained.

`Unnamed: 0` was identified as an identifier and excluded from the
model features.

### Q4. Why didn't you automatically remove outliers?

**Answer:**
An IQR outlier is not automatically a data error. I investigated the
observations first. Since there was no evidence of an error or
implausible value, I retained them and planned to assess their
influence during regression diagnostics.

### Q5. What would you do if you found a negative advertising value?

**Answer:**
Advertising expenditure logically cannot be negative, but I would not
delete it immediately. I would first verify the source and investigate
whether it was a data-entry or transformation error. If confirmed
invalid and uncorrectable, I would then consider removing it and
document the decision.

### Key principle

**Observe → Investigate → Reason → Decide → Document**

Don't automatically delete observations just because they look unusual.


## Stage 3 — EDA & Hypothesis Formation

### Q1. How did you explore the relationships?

**Answer:**
I used bivariate analysis, scatterplots, correlation analysis, and a
correlation heatmap.

### Q2. What did you observe?

**Answer:**
- TV → strongest positive, approximately linear association with Sales.
- Radio → positive association, weaker than TV.
- Newspaper → weaker and more dispersed relationship with Sales.
- Predictor correlations were relatively low.

### Q3. Did you find multicollinearity?

**Answer:**
There was no evidence of strong **pairwise** multicollinearity based on
the correlation matrix. However, correlation alone cannot rule out
multicollinearity, so I planned to assess it formally using VIF.

### Key distinction

Correlation matrix → pairwise relationships

VIF → formal assessment of multicollinearity


## Stage 4 — Correlation vs Causation

### Q1. Does strong TV-Sales correlation mean TV advertising causes sales to increase?

**Answer:**
No. The correlation shows a strong positive linear association, but this
is observational data, so we cannot conclude causation.

Other factors could influence both advertising expenditure and sales.

### Q2. Does strong correlation prove linear regression is appropriate?

**Answer:**
No. It gives us a reason to investigate a linear model, but we still need
to assess model assumptions using residual and regression diagnostics.

### Key distinction

Correlation → association

Regression → estimates relationships / makes predictions

Causation → requires stronger evidence and appropriate study design

Strong correlation ≠ causation


### Q1. Why did you start with simple regression?

**Answer:**
I started with simple linear regression to understand the relationship
between an individual predictor and Sales before introducing multiple
predictors.

TV showed the strongest approximately linear association during EDA, so
I used it as the first model.

It provided an interpretable baseline before moving to multiple regression.

### Q2. What does the TV coefficient of 0.0475 mean?

**Answer:**
A one-unit increase in TV advertising expenditure is associated with an
estimated 0.0475-unit increase in predicted Sales, on average.

This coefficient represents the slope of the regression line.

### Regression equation

Sales_hat = 7.0326 + 0.0475 × TV

- 0.0475 → slope / TV coefficient
- 7.0326 → intercept
- TV → predictor
- Sales_hat → predicted Sales

### Important

Because this is observational data:

**Coefficient ≠ causal effect**

Use:

"associated with an increase"

rather than:

"causes an increase."

### Key idea

Simple regression helps us:

- Understand one predictor's relationship with the target
- Interpret the coefficient
- Test statistical significance
- Establish a baseline
- Compare against the later multiple regression


## Stage 6 — Hypothesis Testing

### Q1. What were you testing?

**Answer:**

H₀: β₁ = 0
H₁: β₁ ≠ 0

We tested whether the population TV coefficient was statistically
different from zero.

### Q2. What does p < 0.001 mean?

**Answer:**

Assuming the null hypothesis is true, observing a test statistic this
extreme or more extreme would be very unlikely.

Therefore, we reject H₀.

### Q3. What can we conclude?

**Answer:**

There is strong statistical evidence of a positive, non-zero linear
association between TV advertising expenditure and Sales.

### Q4. Does statistical significance mean TV is a good predictor?

**Answer:**

Not necessarily.

Statistical significance answers an inference question:

"Is the coefficient different from zero?"

Predictive performance is evaluated using:

- RMSE
- MAE
- R²
- Cross-validation

### Q5. Does statistical significance prove causation?

**Answer:**

No.

The data is observational, so the result shows association, not
causation.

### Common p-value trap

Incorrect:
"There is a <0.1% probability that H₀ is true."

Correct:
"Assuming H₀ is true, a result this extreme would be very unlikely."

## Stage 7 — Train/Test Evaluation

### Q1. Why use a train/test split?

**Answer:**
To evaluate how well the model generalizes to unseen observations.

### Q2. Why not evaluate on training data?

**Answer:**
The model has already seen the training observations, so training
performance can be overly optimistic.

### Q3. What does the test set tell us?

**Answer:**
It provides an estimate of out-of-sample predictive performance.

### Q4. What metrics did we use?

- MAE → average absolute prediction error
- MSE → squared error; penalizes large errors more
- RMSE → prediction error in Sales units
- R² → proportion of target variation explained by the model

### Important distinction

Statistical significance → evidence about a coefficient

Test-set evaluation → predictive generalization

## Stage 8 — Multiple Linear Regression

### Q1. Why did multiple regression improve performance?

**Answer:**
TV alone could not capture all the information related to Sales.
Adding Radio and Newspaper provided additional information and
substantially improved predictive performance.

Test performance:

TV only:
RMSE = 3.194
R²   = 0.677

All three:
RMSE = 1.782
R²   = 0.899

### Q2. Why not keep adding variables?

**Answer:**
R² can increase as predictors are added, especially on training data.
More variables can increase complexity and cause overfitting.

Therefore, model selection should consider:
- Test performance
- Cross-validation
- RMSE / MAE / R²
- Statistical evidence
- Diagnostics
- Model simplicity

### Q3. Interpret Radio coefficient = 0.1892

**Answer:**
Holding TV and Newspaper constant, a one-unit increase in Radio
advertising expenditure is associated with an estimated 0.1892-unit
increase in predicted Sales, on average.

This is an association, not causation.

### Q4. Interpret Newspaper coefficient = 0.0028, p = 0.696

**Answer:**
After accounting for TV and Radio, there is not strong statistical
evidence of a linear association between Newspaper expenditure and Sales.

The confidence interval includes zero.

However, p-value alone should not determine feature selection.

## Stage 9 — Cross-Validation

### Q1. Why use cross-validation after train/test split?

**Answer:**
A single train/test split can produce split-dependent performance.
Cross-validation evaluates the model across multiple validation folds
and gives a more robust estimate of generalization performance.

### Q2. What does mean CV RMSE represent?

**Answer:**
The average validation RMSE across the folds.

It estimates typical prediction error on unseen validation observations.

### Q3. What does CV standard deviation represent?

**Answer:**
How much model performance varies across the validation folds.

### Q4. Can CV RMSE guarantee real-world performance?

**Answer:**
No.

It is an estimate based on the available dataset. Future real-world
performance can differ because of sampling variation, dataset size,
data distribution, and other factors.

### Our result

TV + Radio:
Mean CV RMSE = 1.663

TV + Radio + Newspaper:
Mean CV RMSE = 1.682

Therefore, Newspaper did not provide meaningful predictive improvement
in our cross-validation comparison.

### Key distinction

Train/test split → one holdout estimate

Cross-validation → multiple validation estimates

Neither guarantees future real-world performance.
